\begin{abstract}
We join 332.9 million 30-second lane readings from about 790 Hong Kong road detectors with
hourly district rainfall and rainstorm warning signals over three wet-season months (May 2024,
July and August 2025, including all four 2025 Black Rainstorm episodes), through a three-layer
pipeline whose every cleaning rule is recorded with evidence and counts. Speed falls steadily
with the rain in the detector's district (about 3.4\,\% per 10\,mm/h), while the warning level
mainly changes demand: under Black, flow is 62\,\% of normal whatever the local rain and speed
is higher than under Red. A per-detector sensitivity ranking is stable across storms
(split-half Spearman 0.84) but partly reflects how much rain each district received; after
adjusting, Kowloon urban corridors are the most sensitive. Holding out whole storms, LightGBM
reduces the error of a time-only baseline on wet slots by 28\,\%, almost all of it from rain
features. An ablation of eleven preprocessing steps shows that the lane-to-detector speed
aggregation, the time slot and the rain assignment change the answers, a robust outlier rule
removes real congestion, and the validity flag, stuck sensors and gap filling hardly matter.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Heavy rain is a recurring disruption in Hong Kong. The Observatory's Black Rainstorm
warning itself warns that persistent heavy rain ``will cause serious road flooding and traffic
congestion'', and 2025 was the first year under the current system with four Black Rainstorm
episodes~\cite{arxiv2025black}. That rain slows traffic is well established; what a traffic
manager or a commuter needs is \emph{where} the effect is largest and whether it can be
anticipated. Both questions require joining data that were never designed to be joined:
30-second lane readings from about 790 road detectors, an hourly rainfall \emph{range} per
district published as a weather bulletin, and warning signals issued for the whole territory.

This project treats that join, and the cleaning around it, as the object of study. We
(i) build a reproducible three-layer pipeline (raw as written, cleaned per source, analysis
table) over three wet-season months (May 2024, July and August 2025; 332.9 million lane
readings), recording every cleaning rule with its evidence and counts;
(ii) rank detectors by rainstorm sensitivity (RQ1); (iii) predict the 15-minute speed ratio
during rain events against a time-only baseline, holding out whole storms (RQ2); and
(iv) measure how much each preprocessing choice changes the RQ1 ranking and the RQ2 accuracy
(RQ3).

The main findings are: speed falls steadily with the rain \emph{at the detector} (about
3.4\,\% per 10\,mm/h), while the territory-wide warning level mostly changes \emph{demand}:
under Black the median flow is 62\,\% of normal whatever the local rain, and speed is higher
than under Red at equal rain (a ``Black paradox''). A warning-based sensitivity ranking is
stable across storms (split-half Spearman 0.84) but half of its district pattern is
\emph{exposure} (how much rain fell there); after adjusting, the Kowloon urban corridors
remain the most sensitive roads. Rain features cut the prediction error on wet slots by
28\,\% relative to the time-only baseline, while the warning level adds little. Of eleven
preprocessing steps, the ones that change the answers are how lane readings become a detector
speed (the ranking keeps only 11 of its top 20), the time slot (prediction skill 0.22 to 0.38)
and how rain is assigned to a road; a robust outlier rule quietly removes a quarter of the
rain congestion, and the validity flag, stuck sensors and gap filling hardly matter.


\section{Related Work}
\emph{Rain and speed on a single road.} A study of one Hong Kong urban
road~\cite{ijgi2021rain} reports speed reductions of about 4.2\,\%, 6.3\,\% and 7.3\,\% under
light, moderate and heavy rain. \emph{Rain and city-level congestion.} Work on Guangzhou and
Shenzhen~\cite{frontiers2026rain} models the nonlinear relation between daily rainfall, a
city-wide congestion index and metro ridership. \emph{The 2025 Black Rainstorms} are analysed
meteorologically in~\cite{arxiv2025black}. Prior work thus uses either one road or a daily
city-wide index; we work at detector level across the territory, at 15-minute resolution,
conditioned on official warning levels, and we make the preprocessing itself the variable.
We do not claim novelty for the finding that rain slows traffic.

S1 and S3 come from the DATA.GOV.HK Historical Archive, which stores a copy of the live file
each time it fetched it. This shapes the data more than any sensor does. \textbf{Missing
periods:} the archive did not fetch every minute; 27.0\,\%, 46.6\,\% and 41.0\,\% of the 30-s
periods are absent in the three months (at most 35\,min in a row), so missing data are
\emph{absent rows}, not NA values. \textbf{Identical copies} of the same file are stored at
several fetch times and must be deduplicated. \textbf{Midnight:} the 00:00:00 and 00:00:30
periods carry the previous day's date. \textbf{Three truncated files} are byte-prefixes of
complete copies fetched minutes apart. Inside S1, three quirks matter for the analysis:
(a) with volume 0 the reported speed is the \emph{speed limit} (70, 80, 100, 50 or 110\,km/h
in 99.9\,\% of cases), not a measurement; (b) \texttt{valid = N} readings (2.9\,\%) look normal,
so only the flag identifies them; (c) speed 0 with volume $>0$ occurs in 0.17\,\% of readings
with a mean of 11.3 vehicles per 30\,s and occupancy 36\,\%, physically impossible as a
standstill; (d) speeds above 130\,km/h (up to 300) occur in 0.56\,\%. Volume is 0 in
20.2\,\% of readings. In S3 the rainfall of a district is a range (e.g.\ ``10 to 20\,mm'') over several
gauges, a district with no rain is simply not listed, and three hourly bulletins are missing.

\section{Design and Implementation}
\subsection{Pipeline overview}
The pipeline has three layers, each written to Parquet and processed with
DuckDB~\cite{raasveldt2019duckdb} (the S1 layer does not fit in the 16\,GB of memory we used).
\textbf{L1} parses every raw file \emph{as written}: all values stay strings, nothing is
trimmed, dropped or renamed, and structure checks (field sets, value forms, duplicates) run
here. \textbf{L2} cleans each source separately by explicit rules; every rule has an ID, the
evidence behind it, who decided it, and its row counts in a cleaning record. \textbf{L3} joins
the sources into one analysis table, one row per detector and 15-minute slot; each of its
steps has a default and alternatives (P1--P11) so that RQ3 can rebuild it with one choice
changed (about one minute per build).

Two principles, agreed with the course teacher, guide L2. First, \emph{fail loudly}: a case
that no rule covers raises an error with the offending rows instead of being passed through
or given a default, so a wrong assumption stops the pipeline where it is wrong. Second,
\emph{no NA in L2, with the method chosen by the kind of problem}: a few missing values with
trustworthy neighbours are estimated (and flagged), a value given twice is averaged (and
flagged), and rare bad data in a large source are dropped, after checking that the dropped
rows do not cluster in rain periods.

\subsection{L2 cleaning rules}
Table~\ref{tab:l2} lists the rules that change data. Beyond these, L2 types every field,
trims spaces (2.9\,M S1 readings had a trailing space in \texttt{direction}), and renames
repeated lane names (\texttt{Middle Lane} twice in one detector, 98\,k blocks) by position.
Two rules came from checks failing: S1 \texttt{occupancy = -1} occurs 7,687 times, always with
volume 0 (set to 0); and three tropical-cyclone signals from the 1950s had a duration one hour
longer than end minus start. All three start with an undocumented flag \texttt{S} and end with
\texttt{X}; reading \texttt{S} as Hong Kong summer time (used until 1979) makes all 1,262
durations consistent, so \texttt{S} times are shifted to HKT.

The estimate for TDS90036 uses the detector's own hour-of-day ratios from its complete blocks

remove rain periods preferentially.

\subsection{L3: the analysis table}
Table~\ref{tab:l3} gives the L3 steps. Two defaults differ from our proposal, both for reasons
found in the data. \textbf{P2:} the proposal filtered outliers with a per-detector robust
z-score; but the slow-downs this project studies \emph{are} outliers relative to a detector's
normal speed, so the default keeps only physical bounds and the z-score becomes an RQ3
alternative. \textbf{P10:} a baseline per month left 91\,\% of July 2025 Saturday slots without
three dry reference slots (4 Saturdays, 2 under a cyclone signal); pooling July and August
(both school summer holidays) and treating signals below 8 as dry fixes this. The speed of a
slot is volume-weighted (P4), which gives the placeholder speeds of empty lanes weight 0.

The resulting table has 6,430,886 rows (784 detectors). The main quantity is the
\emph{speed ratio}: the slot's speed divided by the detector's dry-weather median for the same
season, day type (weekday, Saturday, Sunday or holiday) and time of day. The flow ratio is
defined in the same way. A slot is \emph{dry} if its district had no rain in that hour and the
hour before, no rainstorm warning and a cyclone signal below 8.

\subsection{Methods}
\textbf{RQ1.} Per detector, $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ is the median speed ratio under Red or Black
minus 1 (Red and Black pooled; Black alone is four episodes) and $s_{\mathrm{rain}}$ the same
with district rain $\geq 10$\,mm/h. Because districts received different amounts of rain,
we also compute an exposure-adjusted $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ (removing the linear dependence on
the median district rain while Red/Black was in force) and a per-detector rain slope from a
mixed model, ratio $\sim$ rain $+ (1+\mathrm{rain}\,|\,\mathrm{detector})$, on 300\,k wet
slots. Stability is tested by splitting the rain events into two halves.
\textbf{RQ2.} On the 42 event days (a warning or $\geq 10$\,mm/h somewhere; consecutive days
merged into 22 events) we predict the speed ratio and a congestion flag (ratio $<0.7$). The
baseline predicts ratio 1 (the dry profile for that detector and time); we compare ridge /
logistic regression and LightGBM~\cite{ke2017lightgbm} with four feature sets. Folds are
grouped by event, so a storm is never in both training and test data. \textbf{RQ3.} Each
alternative in Table~\ref{tab:l3} is applied alone; we report the Spearman correlation of
$s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ with the default ranking, the overlap of the 20 most sensitive detectors,
the headline ratios, and the LightGBM skill $1-\mathrm{MAE}/\mathrm{MAE}_{\mathrm{baseline}}$
(the target itself changes with the variant, so skill is the comparable measure).

\section{Evaluation}
\subsection{Exploration: rain, warnings, speed and flow}
Fig.~\ref{fig:events} shows the rain and warnings of the three months. Table~\ref{tab:levels}
and Fig.~\ref{fig:rain} summarise the speed and flow ratios (cyclone signal $\geq 8$ left
out). Speed falls monotonically with the rain in the detector's district: without a warning
from 0.976 at 0--5\,mm/h to 0.888 above 40\,mm/h, and under a warning from 0.951 to 0.825.
By warning level, however, the ordering breaks: the median speed ratio is 0.940 under Amber,
0.918 under Red but 0.937 under Black. The flow ratio explains why: it is 0.93 and 0.90 under
Amber and Red but 0.62 under Black, and it stays near 0.62 in every rain bin, even where the
district had no rain at all (where the Black speed ratio is 1.007, faster than normal).
At equal local rain, speed under Black is never below that under Amber or Red up to
40\,mm/h, and above Red in every bin (Fig.~\ref{fig:rain}, left); only above 40\,mm/h is
Black the slowest.

The 5~August 2025 Black Rainstorm (Fig.~\ref{fig:black}) shows the mechanism in time. The
Black signal was issued at 05:50; at 06:00 the median speed ratio reached its minimum of 0.77
while the morning traffic was already on the road. Flow then collapsed to 0.38 of normal by
about 09:00 and speed recovered to 1.0 by 16:00, while Black was still in force. After the
signal was cancelled at 17:05, flow stayed near half of normal through the evening. We read
this as a demand response to the Black signal (under the Labour Department's code of practice,
employees are generally not required to report for work~\cite{labour_code}); see
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} for the hypotheses and checks.

\subsection{RQ1: sensitivity ranking}
781 detectors have at least 20 Red/Black slots. Their $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ has median
$-0.075$ (interquartile range $-0.114$ to $-0.044$); the most sensitive detector falls by
21\,\%. The ranking is a stable property of the road: $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ and
$s_{\mathrm{rain}}$ correlate at $\rho=0.93$, and computed on two disjoint halves of the rain
events the rankings correlate at 0.84 ($s_{\mathrm{warn}}$) and 0.90 ($s_{\mathrm{rain}}$).
But $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$ also correlates at $-0.51$ with the rain the detector's district
actually received while Red or Black was in force (median 5\,mm/h in North District,
14.5\,mm/h on the Islands). Half of the ``district pattern'' of a warning-based ranking is
therefore exposure, not sensitivity. After adjusting (Fig.~\ref{fig:rq1}), the Kowloon urban
districts (Yau Tsim Mong, Kowloon City, Sham Shui Po, Kwun Tong) remain the most sensitive;
North District moves up and the Islands move down (their expressways had the most rain but
fall least per mm). 13 of the 20 most sensitive detectors are the same before and after
adjustment; they are urban corridors and their approaches: Tseung Kwan O Road, Kwun Tong
Bypass, Lung Cheung Road, West Kowloon Corridor, Chatham Road North and Lion Rock Tunnel
Road. The mixed model gives a mean slope of $-0.034$ speed ratio per 10\,mm/h (s.e.\ 0.001)
with a between-detector standard deviation of 0.035: the slope itself varies as much as its
mean. Baseline occupancy correlates \emph{positively} with $s_{\mathrm{warn}}$
($\rho=+0.25$): busier roads lose \emph{less} of their normal speed.

\subsection{RQ2: prediction during rain events}
The 42 event days hold 2.83\,M slots; 1.1\,\% are congested (ratio $<0.7$), 3.9\,\% under
Red/Black. Table~\ref{tab:rq2} gives the five-fold, event-held-out results. LightGBM with rain
and warning features reduces the MAE on wet slots from 0.079 (baseline) to 0.057, a 28\,\%
reduction, and on Red/Black slots from 0.111 to 0.072 ($-36$\,\%). The feature sets separate
the two kinds of information: rain features alone reach 0.057, warning features alone only
0.067, and adding warnings to rain changes the MAE by 0.0006. In the gain importance the
district rain of the hour (35\,\%) and of the hour before (24\,\%) dominate; the warning level
has 2\,\%. Congestion as a class is hard: F1 stays at 0.14--0.17 for all models, far below
what the regression gain would suggest.

\subsection{RQ3: how much does preprocessing matter?}
\label{sec:rq3}

default: the L3 table is rebuilt (75--160\,s each), RQ1 is recomputed, and LightGBM with all

target itself changes with the variant, we compare \emph{skill} ($1-\mathrm{MAE}/\mathrm{MAE}$

\emph{How speed is measured matters most.} The lane-to-detector aggregation (P4) is the only
step that reorders the ranking substantially: with either alternative only 11 of the 20 most
sensitive detectors remain. Averaging all readings lets the empty-lane readings, whose
``speed'' is the posted limit, dilute every ratio towards 1 (Black 0.961 instead of 0.937) and
halves the congested share; taking the slowest lane does the opposite (6.2\,\% congested,
higher F1, lower skill). \emph{The time resolution changes what can be predicted}: skill is
0.215 at 5\,min, 0.278 at 15\,min and 0.378 at 60\,min, because the rain input is hourly and a
longer slot averages out the variation that rain cannot explain; the ranking still keeps 15 of
20. \emph{The rain assignment matters for prediction only}: the territory maximum instead of the
own district's rain lowers skill by a quarter (0.278 to 0.212), and the rain of the hour before
instead of the current hour lowers it to 0.233; the upper bound of the range is as good as the
midpoint. Warning features, by contrast, change skill by 0.006 when removed, though they help
the congestion classifier (F1 0.168 to 0.144). \emph{An outlier rule can remove the
phenomenon}: the robust $z$ rule (P2) raises skill to 0.290, but the 1.73\,M readings it adds to
the exclusions are mostly slow (72\,\% below the lane median; median 20\,km/h on lanes with a
median of 72\,km/h) and over-represented under Red and Black (1.7 times their share of the
kept readings), so it removes a quarter of the congestion under Red/Black (3.9 to 3.0\,\%) and
F1 falls. \emph{The steps most discussed during cleaning matter least}: the validity flag (P1),
stuck sensors (P3), gap interpolation (P6) and the cyclone filter (P11) all keep
$\rho\geq0.998$ and change skill by less than the noise. The baseline (P10) is in between: a
single baseline for all three months keeps 16 of the top 20.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
Several results differ from what we expected. We state them as hypotheses with the check that
would confirm or reject each; none is verified yet.

\textbf{H1: the Black paradox is a demand effect.} Under Black the speed ratio is higher than
under Red because fewer vehicles travel: flow is 0.62 of normal in every rain bin, including
districts without rain, and keeps falling with time since the signal (0.95 in the first
30\,min, 0.52 after 8\,h). \emph{Against:} there are only four Black episodes, three on
weekdays, and 5 August dominates. \emph{Check:} per-episode curves; weekday vs weekend
episodes; a regression of speed ratio on rain, flow ratio and level.

\textbf{H2: the warning level is a territory-wide label; local rain drives speed.} At equal
district rain the levels differ by at most 0.03 in speed ratio below 40\,mm/h, and in RQ2 the
warning features add almost nothing to rain features. RQ3 agrees: replacing the district's
rain by the territory maximum, which is what a territory-wide signal summarises, lowers skill
by a quarter. \emph{Check:} rain of neighbouring districts and of the route; the radar nowcast
at the detector.

\textbf{H3: the speed shock is at warning onset.} On 5 August the minimum speed ratio came 10
minutes after the Black signal, and speed recovered while Black was still in force.
\emph{Against:} pooled over all episodes the speed ratio is flat in time since issue (Amber
0.94 throughout). \emph{Check:} episode curves aligned at issue time, weekday mornings only.

\textbf{H4: a warning adds a slow-down beyond the district rain} (at 20--40\,mm/h: 0.926
without, 0.870 with a warning): either rain elsewhere on the route, or the hourly district
range misses short intense bursts. \emph{Check:} add neighbouring districts' rain; S7 radar
nowcast at 6-min resolution.

\textbf{H5: sensitive roads are near capacity, yet busier roads lose less in ratio}
($\rho=+0.25$ with baseline occupancy). A ratio has less room to fall where the dry baseline
is already slow, and demand relief helps busy roads most. \emph{Check:} rank on the speed
difference in km/h instead of the ratio; road class from the road network data.

\textbf{H6: congestion is local.} Speed ratio $<0.7$ is predicted with F1 0.14--0.17 although
the MAE improves by 28\,\%; incidents, flooded lanes or closures would explain this.
\emph{Check:} join the Transport Department's special traffic news to the congested slots.

\textbf{H7: stuck presence readings mark flooding or closures.} All 1,122 lane slots removed
by P3 read occupancy 100 with speed and volume 0; the day with most is 20 July 2025, under
cyclone signal 10 (10 detectors). \emph{Check:} traffic news for those roads.

\textbf{H8: robust outlier rules delete rain congestion.} The $z$ rule's extra exclusions are
slow readings concentrated in warning periods (Section~\ref{sec:rq3}). A per-lane
distribution over three months treats a real queue as an outlier. \emph{Check:} compare the
excluded readings with the neighbouring lanes and detectors in the same 30\,s; a conditional
rule (outlier relative to the same slot of day, or only when the neighbours disagree).

\textbf{H9: the P4 alternatives reorder the ranking through the empty-lane placeholder and
lane mix.} Mean-of-readings dilutes by the share of empty lanes, which is highest on quiet
roads and at night; slowest-lane follows the slow or exit lane. \emph{Check:} ranking with a
volume-weighted mean restricted to the same lanes over time; per-lane sensitivity.

\textbf{H10: the gain at 60\,min is resolution matching, not a better model.} Rain is an
hourly district range, so at 15 and 5\,min the target has variation the input cannot see.
\emph{Check:} 15-min targets with the 6-min radar nowcast (S7); skill at 60\,min without rain
features.

\textbf{Limitations.} Three months, four Black episodes; rainfall is an hourly range per
district, not a gauge at the road; the archive misses 27--47\,\% of the 30-s periods; the
baseline assumes the dry days of a season are comparable; we have no incident data in the
model.

\section{Conclusion}
Joining detector, rainfall and warning data at 15-minute detector level shows that where it
rains matters more than which signal is hoisted: speed follows the district's rain, the
warning level mostly changes how many people travel, and a sensitivity ranking built on
warnings must be adjusted for exposure before it says anything about the road. With that
adjustment the Kowloon urban corridors stand out. Rain features make storm-time speed
predictable in aggregate, but congestion episodes remain largely unexplained. For the
preprocessing, the lesson of the ablation is that the consequential choices are those that
define the quantity being measured (speed aggregation, slot length, rain assignment, what
counts as an outlier), not the ones that decide which few readings to keep. The next steps are
the checks listed with H1--H10: per-episode analysis of the Black paradox, traffic news for
congestion and stuck sensors, radar rainfall at the detector, and more months.
